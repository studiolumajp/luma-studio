Shimoda and the black ships of 1854

At the southern end of the Izu Peninsula, Shimoda is where Japan's opening to the West began. Up to six people, on foot and by ropeway.

Overview

Where the treaty was signed

Commodore Perry's fleet came to Shimoda in 1854. Perry walked to Ryosen-ji with about 300 men to sign the Shimoda treaty. The lane they marched along is now called Perry Road, around 500 metres of stone paving lined with traditional namako walls.

Five minutes from the station, Hofuku-ji keeps a photo of Okichi taken in 1857, when she was 19. It also keeps the room where, in 1863, Katsu Kaishu asked the lord Yamauchi Yodo to pardon Sakamoto Ryoma.

Shimoda lands more kinmedai (splendid alfonsino) than any other port in Japan, over 1,000 tonnes a year. That settles lunch.

Highlights

  • Walk Perry Road, the lane Perry's men marched along in 1854
  • Stand in the 1863 audience room at Hofuku-ji
  • Ride the ropeway up Mt Nesugata, a minute's walk from the station
  • Eat kinmedai, the fish Shimoda lands more of than anywhere else in Japan
  • See more than 3,000 items on Japan's opening at the MoBS Black Ship Museum

FAQ

Questions people ask

Why does Shimoda matter?

Japan's opening to the West began here. Perry's fleet arrived in 1854, the Shimoda treaty was signed at Ryosen-ji, and Yoshida Shoin tried to board the black ships from near Benten Island.

What is kinmedai?

It's the fish Shimoda is known for. About 80% of the fish landed here is kinmedai, and you'll find it in more than 30 different dishes around town.
